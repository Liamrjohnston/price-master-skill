# Rate maths

Every formula here uses only the owner's own figures. Show the working in the card. Label each figure (yours), (calc) or (estimate).

## 1. Effective rate per client

```
effective rate = what the client pays in a period / hours actually spent on them in that period
```

- Use the same period for both (a month is easiest). A $650 monthly fee for 10 hours is $65/hr. A $300 monthly fee for 18 hours is $16.67/hr.
- Include the hidden hours: travel, calls, revisions, chasing paperwork, admin for that client. If the owner gave a total but not the split, say so and estimate the split, labelled.
- Sort lowest first. The bottom few usually tell the story.

**Blended rate** = total revenue / total hours worked. Compare it with the rate the owner thinks they charge. The gap is the cost of the low-rate clients.

**Share of hours** for the bottom group = their hours / total hours. If 3 clients take a third of the hours and pay a fraction of the revenue, the first fix is those 3 clients, not a raise for everyone.

## 2. The floor

```
floor rate = (what the owner wants to take home a year + business costs a year + a buffer) / realistic billable hours a year
```

- If the owner didn't give a take-home target, don't choose one. Work the floor from what they earn now, or show the formula with [N] for them to fill in.
- Realistic billable hours: the hours they actually bill, not 40 hours times 52 weeks. Take out holidays, sick days, admin, sales and travel. If they don't know, use their recent actual hours and label it.
- Tax: if they mention tax, include it in the take-home or buffer as they describe. Don't invent a tax rate; use [N]% if needed and say to check with their accountant.
- Work the floor once, in one line of arithmetic. Don't revise it mid-answer; if an input is unknown, write [N] instead.
- The floor is the minimum, not the answer. Price is set by value and capacity above it. Cost is never the argument to clients.

## 3. Costs and margin (businesses with staff or materials)

Rebuild the real cost of the work before trusting any margin:

```
real labour cost per hour of work = wage x (1 + payroll extras %) x (paid hours / hours on the job)
```

- Payroll extras: whatever the owner pays on top of wages (employer taxes, insurance, holiday pay), as they describe it.
- Paid hours vs hours on the job: if staff are paid for drive time or setup, the real cost per hour of cleaning, building or serving is higher than the wage.
- Add supplies, fuel and anything else that scales with the work.

```
margin = (revenue - costs of doing the work - overheads) / revenue
```

**Cost increase as a share of revenue** = the extra yearly cost / current yearly revenue. A price rise smaller than this leaves the owner worse off than before. Say so with the numbers.

## 4. Break-even: how much work can you lose and still be ahead?

Let `p` = the price rise as a fraction (20% = 0.20) and `m` = the **contribution margin** on that work before the rise, as a fraction:

```
m = (price - the costs that leave when the client leaves) / price
```

Costs that leave with the client: wages for the hours on that job (with payroll extras and paid travel), supplies, fuel for that job. Costs that stay whether or not the client stays (rent, insurance, software, the owner's own pay) are **not** taken off. Using net profit margin here is a mistake: it makes the break-even look far bigger than it is.

```
share of work you can lose and keep the same contribution = p / (m + p)
```

- **Solo, time-based work** (nothing leaves with the client): `m` = 1, so it is `p / (1 + p)`. A 20% rise: 0.20 / 1.20 = 16.7% of the work could go and revenue stays level. Say also that the freed hours can be refilled at the new rate.
- **With staff or materials**: at a 35% contribution margin, a 10% rise: 0.10 / (0.35 + 0.10) = 22.2% of the work could go and contribution stays level. Staff hours must be cut to match. Say both.
- Use the effective rate the client really pays, not the rate the owner quotes, as the starting price.
- Turn the share into clients the owner recognises: "you could lose Client X and Client Y and still be ahead".

## 5. Before and after, three cases

Always show monthly and yearly, with working. Build each row line by line before totalling: list each client (or group) with hours x new rate, sum them, then multiply by 12. If a case refills freed hours, show hours x rate for the refill separately. Recheck each row from scratch once.

1. **Everyone stays** at the new prices.
2. **Likely leavers go**: the clients the owner named as price-sensitive, or the release candidates, leave. Show their freed hours too.
3. **Worst realistic case for the big client**: if one client is about 25% of revenue or more, show the year if they leave, and what it would take to replace them.

Never present case 1 as the forecast.

## 6. Concentration

```
client share = that client's yearly revenue / total yearly revenue
```

- About 25% or more: protect (conversation, own timing, often phased).
- 40% or more: the price rise is secondary to the risk. A phased rise at renewal plus a plan to win other clients is usually wiser than a big step now. Say so.

## 7. Arithmetic check

Before writing the card:
- Sum the client fees and check them against the owner's stated revenue. If they differ, say which you used.
- Check one effective rate by hand in the card so the owner can follow it.
- Recompute the yearly figure from the monthly one (x 12) and from the per-client totals. They must match.
- Check every percentage against its two numbers.
